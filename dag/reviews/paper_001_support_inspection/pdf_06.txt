8.   Halting  Pitfalls  and  Zeno’s  Trap
8.1  The  Counterexample:   Strict  Descent  Without  Termination
Consider a single edge with report 𝑐  = 1, weight 𝑤  = 1.  Suppose an optimization
routine  generates  a  sequence  of  potential  differences  such  that  the  cost  at  step
𝑡 is:


                                               𝐷𝑡  = 2−𝑡

Notice:  1.  Strict  Monotonic  Descent:  At every single step, 𝐷𝑡+1  = 2−(𝑡+1)<
2−𝑡   =  𝐷𝑡.    2.    Infinite   Rational   Sequence:   The  sequence  strictly  decreases
for  all  𝑡 ∈ ℕ without  ever  reaching  the  infimum  0 at  any  finite  𝑡.

The  Cardinal  Halting  Law:
       Strict  rational  descent  alone  does  NOT  imply  finite  termi-
       nation.
To  make  a  stopping  argument,  state  which  quantity  is  bounded  and  which  calls
must  return:
   •   If  all  costs  lie  on  a  fixed  lattice  𝛿ℤ  with  𝛿  >  0,  are  bounded  below,  and
       each admitted step strictly decreases cost, there are finitely many admitted
       descents from a finite starting cost.   Rejected attempts still need their own
       limit.
   •   A   finite   exhaustive   search   terminates   if   it   visits   its   finite   candidate   set
       without  repetition  and  every  candidate  check  returns.   Searching  extreme
       points  without  these  conditions  is  not  a  halting  proof.
   •   A finite fuel wrapper bounds attempted transitions.  A timeout or a proved
       bound  is  separately  needed  for  each  parser,  solver,  or  external  call.   Fuel
       exhaustion  means  an  unfinished  computation,  not  convergence.
